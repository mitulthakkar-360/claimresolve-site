02 / Their playbook

Eight sentences. Your rejection is one of them.

Rejection letters read as though they were written for your case alone. In practice almost every refusal in India is built from the same short list. Find yours below — and read what sits behind it.

01

“The claim was filed beyond the permitted period.”

Delay is a question they have to answer, not just ask.

An insurer citing delay has to show the delay actually caused it harm. Most rejection letters assert the delay and stop there — which is precisely the gap an argued representation is built on.

Frequently contestable
02

“The treatment falls under an exclusion in the policy.”

An exclusion is only as wide as its own wording.

We go back to the exact clause, to the schedule, and to what was disclosed to you at sale. Exclusions that were never properly pointed out to you, or that do not cover your facts, are the ones that fall.

Frequently contestable
03

“You did not disclose a pre-existing disease.”

What were you actually asked — and when was it recorded?

A single stray remark in one hospital file is not the same as a diagnosis, and a blank question on a form the agent filled in for you is not your concealment. This is the single most common ground we overturn.

Frequently contestable
04

“The claim falls within the waiting period.”

Waiting periods get quietly restarted. That restart is arguable.

We check how the period is counted for your policy year, and whether a renewal was used to reset a clock that had already run. Policyholders rarely spot this on their own.

Frequently contestable
05

“Hospitalisation was not medically required.”

Or the procedure is on their own admitted list.

Many plans cover day-care treatment that a claims officer has assessed as outpatient. The insurer's own product document is often the thing that settles this.

Depends on the wording
06

“The hospital is not in the network, or has been blacklisted.”

When did the blacklisting take effect — and was it an emergency?

Emergency admissions at non-network hospitals are treated differently from planned ones, and a blacklisting dated after your admission changes the whole question.

Frequently contestable
07

“The admission was only for diagnosis.”

Investigation, or part of a treatment plan?

The distinction the insurer draws is often not one the record actually supports. We read the discharge summary and the investigator's note together, not separately.

Depends on the wording
08

“Required documents have not been submitted.”

They asked for a longer list than the policy requires.

There is a difference between what the policy and the regulations require and what the letter asked you for. A request used to stall a payable claim is itself a grievance.

Frequently contestable

Which one is yours? Send it to us and we will tell you — in writing, at no cost, before you pay anyone a rupee.

Six of the eight above are, in our experience, grounds that can be argued properly. The other two depend entirely on your policy wording. Either way, you will know which one you are holding.

03 / Where cases are lost

Almost nobody loses at the final stage. They lose in the first reply.

Rejected claims are not usually lost for lack of a good case. They are lost in the first letter the policyholder sends — or in the letters never sent at all. Everything after that is built on what that first reply created.

How a case quietly dies
  • The letter arrives. It is read, felt, and put in a drawer — for weeks that turn into months.
  • A reply is written in anger, listing grievances instead of clauses. It becomes the record.
  • The insurer's answer is accepted as final because it is written in formal language.
  • Papers are misplaced. The hospital's records are no longer easy to obtain.
  • Family says to let it go, because chasing it is exhausting and nobody explains what to do.
  • The one-year window closes — and the strongest forum is simply gone.
What changes when someone is on your side
  • The same letter is read as a legal document — clause by clause, against your policy wording.
  • The first representation is drafted from the policy, not from the hurt. It sets the record you will rely on later.
  • The insurer's reply is put to the test instead of being treated as a decision.
  • The full document set is assembled while it can still be assembled.
  • Every stage is filed on time, by someone whose job is to keep it moving.
  • The file is pushed to the forum that can actually overrule them — before the clock runs out.

Why this cannot simply wait

The Insurance Ombudsman will not normally entertain a complaint unless it is made within one year of the insurer's final reply — or within one month if the insurer never replied at all. That is the forum with the power to overrule your insurer, and it is free to approach. The record you build now is what decides how far your case can travel.

Evidence also decays in a much simpler way: discharge summaries get archived, hospitals change systems, the doctor who signed the record moves on, and the person who filled in your proposal form is impossible to find. A file that is arguable today is a file that is harder to argue in eight months.

  1. Stage 01

    The insurer's grievance officer

    A documented representation argued from the policy wording. This is not a formality — it is the foundation every later stage stands on.

  2. Stage 02

    Bima Bharosa

    The regulator's single-window grievance system, where your complaint is tracked and the insurer answers on a clock it does not control.

  3. Stage 03

    The Insurance Ombudsman

    An independent authority that can overrule the insurer. Awards are binding on the insurer once accepted, on matters up to ₹50 lakh.

  4. Stage 04

    Consumer commission

    For amounts beyond that limit, or where the facts need a judicial finding. We say plainly when a matter has moved past a consultancy.

Ombudsman time limits and jurisdiction are set out under the insurance ombudsman framework — see the Council for Insurance Ombudsmen. The regulator's grievance portal is at bimabharosa.irdai.gov.in. Limits and procedures are revised from time to time; we confirm the position for your file at the review stage.

05 / Mis-selling

You were not careless. You were sold a story.

Mis-selling is a separate grievance with its own remedies — and it does not depend on whether a claim was ever filed. The question is not what your policy turned out to be. It is what you were told it was, on the day you signed.

Pattern 01

Sold as a fixed deposit

A life policy presented as an FD, with a “guaranteed” return that turns out to be a projection printed in a brochure.

Pattern 02

Promised an interest-free loan

A loan against the policy, offered to close the sale and never available on the terms described.

Pattern 03

“We'll recover your lapsed money”

An agent offers to restore premiums already lost — then sells you a fresh policy instead.

Pattern 04

Sold as free health insurance

Bundled cover described as free, which turns out to be neither free nor usable the day you claim.

Pattern 05

Gold coins as an inducement

Gifts used to close a sale the product itself could never justify. Inducements of this kind are not permitted.

Pattern 06

Tower or telecom “installation income”

A scheme promising recurring income from an installation that does not exist.

Pattern 07

Sold as assured regular income

Described as income, when the payout is conditional, delayed, and nowhere near what was sketched on the table.

Pattern 08

Job assurance

Employment promised in exchange for buying a policy. An outright misrepresentation, and one we see repeatedly.

They misled you once to sell the policy. Do not let the same asymmetry take the claim as well.

We also handle single-premium policies sold to senior citizens, unit-linked plans described as mutual funds, riders attached without consent, and cover bundled quietly into a loan without the borrower ever being told it was optional. Every one of these is a grievance with a route — and the route starts with the paperwork you still have.

10 / Documents

Keep these ready. Anything you cannot find, tell us.

A case is decided on paper. The ones who win are the ones who produce the record — which is the same reason you should not spend another six months deciding.

  • Policy document — the schedule and the full wording booklet
  • Copy of the proposal form you signed
  • Premium receipts, or the bank statement showing the payments
  • The rejection, repudiation or short-settlement letter
  • Every message with the insurer or the agent — email, SMS, WhatsApp
  • Hospital discharge summary and itemised bills
  • Investigation reports, scans and test results
  • FIR, charge-sheet or MACT papers, for a motor claim
  • Death certificate, claim forms and nomination details, for a life claim
  • The agent's name, code, and any brochure or written pitch you kept
  • Bank mandate or NACH details on record with the insurer
  • Photo ID and address proof

Missing half of this? That is normal, and it is not a reason to give up. Send what you have — the free read will tell you whether the rest is worth chasing.

What if I cannot afford a lawyer?
You do not need one for the stages that close most cases. The insurer and Bima Bharosa stages are document-led, and the Ombudsman forum itself does not require legal representation. Where a matter genuinely needs counsel — a consumer commission or a court — we tell you, and tell you what that adds, instead of quietly running up charges.
The claim was rejected months ago. Have I already lost it?
Not by itself. The time limits generally run from the insurer's final reply rather than from the treatment or the loss, and the Ombudsman route is open for one year from that reply. That is a real deadline and it moves — but months are not years. Send us the date on the letter and we will tell you which routes are still open before you spend anything.
How much will this cost me?
Every case starts with a consultation, online or offline. In it we read your letter and your policy, tell you whether there is a case, and then tell you exactly what it costs. Nothing is charged before that fee is in writing, and if you would rather not speak to anyone yet, the first read of your letter costs nothing.
Are you an insurance company, a broker or an agent?
None of those. We are an independent claims advisory and consultancy. We do not sell, market, solicit or distribute insurance policies, and we hold no agency for any insurer. That is deliberate — it is why we can argue against an insurer without a conflict, and why our only income from your case is the fee you agreed to.
What if the insurer was actually right to reject the claim?
Then we tell you at the free review, and we do not take the case. A consultancy that accepts every file is not doing you a service — it is charging you for a delay. You lose nothing by asking, and you will know where you stand.
You are not in my city. Does that matter?
No. The insurer and Bima Bharosa stages are document-based and run from anywhere in India, as does an Ombudsman filing — and the consultation itself can be online, by phone or video. Local presence matters only if a matter proceeds to a hearing, and we will tell you clearly when that point arrives.
How long will my case take?
It depends almost entirely on which stage closes it — a representation to the insurer, a Bima Bharosa complaint, or an Ombudsman filing all move at different speeds. At the consultation stage we give you a realistic estimate in writing, rather than a number designed to close a sale.
Can you help while the policy is still running?
Yes. Mis-selling and misrepresentation grievances are usually about a policy that is still in force — the complaint is about how it was sold, and it can be raised while you carry on paying.
What about the family of someone who has died?
A life claim refused after the earning member has gone is the worst version of this and gets handled as such. The family does not have to learn insurance law to be paid what the policy promised. Send us the policy and the repudiation letter and we will take it from there.
